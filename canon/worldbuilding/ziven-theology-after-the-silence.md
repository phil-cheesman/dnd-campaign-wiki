# Ziven Theology After the Silence

How the [Zivens](#) of [Ilharess](#) understand the gods three thousand years after the
[Sundering](#) — drawn from the public shelves of the city's [library](#), which the party was
given the run of at the end of **E167**.

> **Source.** This page is built from Jon's between-sessions research handout (delivered to the
> table 17 Aug 2026), which answers the library research the party declared on the record at the
> end of E167. Everything below is what the *books say*, not a ruling on what is true. Where a
> text is quoted, the quotation is verbatim from the handout.

## The headline

**The Zivens are not taught that the gods became monsters. They are taught that the gods
abandoned a relationship they once shared.**

The recurring word across the whole shelf is **"absent."** Not evil, not wicked, not fallen.
Absent. And the recurring name for the gods is not *masters* and not *creators* but —

> **"Our First Teachers."**

Historian **Vaelir Thesryn** uses that phrase throughout *When Heaven Walked Beside Us*, the most
commonly read history in Ilharess, and the handout notes the distinction "appears deliberately
chosen."

## The lost golden age — *When Heaven Walked Beside Us*

**by Historian Vaelir Thesryn.** A popular history of the earliest Ziven centuries, when the gods
physically dwelt in [Maena](#).

> *There was once no distinction between prayer and conversation.*
>
> *The gods did not answer us from distant heavens. They sat within our halls. They walked our
> markets. They stood beside our teachers and corrected the hand of the student.*
>
> *We knew the sound of their laughter.*
>
> *We knew which of them preferred silence and which would argue until dawn.*
>
> *There are children born today who believe this to be poetry.*
>
> *It is not.*
>
> *It is memory.*

The text calls the divine separation perhaps the greatest cultural loss in Ziven history.

## The argument the party will actually have to answer

Four texts, escalating.

**1. *Children Between Thrones*, by Maeritha Ulven** — a philosophical study that refuses to
assign full blame to either divine faction. Its claim is that the Zivens were *trapped inside a
conflict that began among the gods themselves.*

> *When the heavens divided, they demanded that the earth divide with them.*
>
> *One teacher called another corrupt. One creator condemned the gifts another had bestowed. And
> the children were asked to determine which parent had become wicked.*
>
> *We chose. Some chose poorly. Some chose wisely. Most chose desperately.*
>
> *History remembers the choice. It rarely remembers who demanded that the choice be made.*

Notably, it **concedes that some Zivens sided with the Betrayer Gods.** It simply frames the
larger tragedy as one the gods forced on them.

**2. *The Severed Communion*, by High Theologian Selanar Voss** — a theology of loss. Never
describes the Faithful Gods as evil; the word is always *absent*.

> *The punishment was not exile alone. Exile may be endured. Distance may be crossed. A city may
> be rebuilt. What was taken from us was communion.*
>
> *There are wounds made by swords. There are wounds made by absence. We have endured the latter
> for three thousand years.*

**3. *On Judgment Without Reconciliation*, by Jurist Aethrys Vel** — modern, extremely
influential, and the strongest thing on the shelf, because it **concedes the charge and moves the
argument forward anyway**:

> *Let us concede every accusation. Let us concede that our ancestors were proud. Let us concede
> that terrible experiments were conducted. Let us concede that ambition outran wisdom and that
> innocent lives were lost.*
>
> *Then let the question become more difficult.*
>
> ***What crime has the child committed who is born three thousand years after judgment has been
> passed?***
>
> *What repentance may the unborn offer? What sentence properly descends through a thousand
> generations?*
>
> *Punishment without the possibility of reconciliation ceases eventually to be correction. It
> becomes inheritance.*

**4. *The Faithful Error*, by Scholar Darion Maeve** — the harshest text still on open shelves:

> *The Faithful Gods defeated those whom history now names Betrayers. They then punished an
> entire civilization for failing to distinguish divine wisdom from divine ambition while the
> gods themselves had failed to do precisely that.*
>
> *This is not proof that the Faithful were wicked. It is proof that victory and righteousness
> are not synonymous.*

## The line that should worry the party

**Philosopher Lorenth Ilyvar**, *The Measure of Justice* — widely taught, and where the language
tips:

> *Forgiveness cannot precede remembrance. Reconciliation cannot precede acknowledgment. Peace
> cannot precede justice.*
>
> *We need not hate those who wronged us. Hatred diminishes judgment. But neither should patience
> be mistaken for forgetting.*
>
> *What was taken must someday be accounted for. What was broken must someday be restored. And
> what was denied must someday be returned.*

**The text never defines what "must someday be returned."** Land? Citizenship? Divine favour?
Political sovereignty? All of Maena? Ziven commentators read it differently — which is the point.
See [the Ziven return debate](#).

## They are not monolithic — and they still pray

Two finds cut against the idea of a single Ziven position.

***Letters Beneath a Silent Heaven*, by Ilvara Sen** — personal letters from a first-generation
exile, written roughly **forty years after the Sundering**. It names gods the party knows:

> *Sometimes I remember **Corellon** standing beneath the flowering trees. I remember **Melora**
> laughing when the rain ruined our ceremony. I remember **Bahamut** speaking with my grandfather
> as though an old soldier were worthy of the attention of a god.*
>
> *And then I look upward and there is nothing.*
>
> *We are told to be angry. Some days I am. Mostly I am homesick for people who could cross the
> universe and yet chose not to come.*

That last line is famous in later Ziven literature and is quoted by **Restorationists and
Reconciliationists alike**.

***A Prayer for Those Beyond the Gate*, anonymous** — a short devotional of uncertain date, still
recited by some Ziven families. **Some Zivens still pray.** Not necessarily expecting an answer:

> *To those who taught us: remember. To those who judged us: remember. To those who loved us:
> remember. To those who abandoned us: remember.*
>
> *We remain.*
>
> *When the distance between us closes, may we meet not as gods and prisoners, but as those who
> have endured long enough to speak plainly.*

***Three Thousand Years of Silence*, by Elaris Veyn** — one of the newest works, popular among
younger scholars, and openly impatient with the whole inheritance:

> *I have never seen a god. Neither did my mother. Nor hers. Yet I inherited their quarrel.*
>
> *We maintain temples to teachers we never met, grievances against judges who have never spoken
> to us, and homesickness for a world no living Ziven remembers.*
>
> *Perhaps this is justice. Perhaps it is madness. Perhaps those are not mutually exclusive.*

The essay **is not banned.**

## Why the transition was so violent

***The Last Conversations*, compiled by Archivist Nereth Caldrin** — surviving eyewitness accounts
of Ziven–divine conversations in the final years before the Sundering. Fragmentary, but the
introduction matters:

> *Those who study this period often speak as though the Gods departed gradually. They did not.*
>
> *Within living memory they went from teachers, patrons, adversaries, and companions to distant
> powers divided by war.*
>
> *Imagine that every temple in Alambor suddenly contained not silence but the memory of someone
> who had once answered.*

This is the text that explains how modern Zivens can feel betrayed **and still speak almost
lovingly about individual gods.**

## Why this matters at the table

- The party arrived braced for a civilization of villains and found a **persuasive** one. Aethrys
  Vel's question — what crime has the child committed? — does not require the Zivens to deny
  anything their ancestors did.
- It sharpens the contrast with **[Adune](#)**, who — Jon confirmed in E167 — **blames and hates
  all the gods, Betrayers included.** The city's own public theology is markedly gentler than its
  most famous surviving son. (?)
- **Erathis/Arethus, Corellon, Melora, Bahamut** are all named as gods the Zivens personally knew.
  [Berrian](#)'s Melora thread and the [God Isles](#) run straight through this material.
